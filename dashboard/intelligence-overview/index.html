    Intelligence Overview (home)¶

    The Intelligence Overview is the landing page for the selected pathogen — a one-screen summary designed for a quick read: what did the analysis find, how much confidence does it deserve, and where do I look next?

    This walkthrough uses an influenza run (H3N2) as the example:

    Reading top to bottom, the page carries four things: the assessment badges (the verdict), the situation strip (what was submitted), the narrative (a written summary), and the evidence cards (one per analytical layer, each a doorway into a full page). The card header itself — Intelligence Brief — h3n2 plus the family badge — is also the species dropdown, kept in sync with the navbar switcher described in Navigation & species selection.

    Step 1 — Read the assessment badges¶

    At the top of the card, a row of badges compresses the whole analysis into a verdict. In the screenshot above they read MONITOR · Confidence: High · Transmission risk: low · Narrative: ollama, followed by the generation timestamp:

    • Assessment — MONITOR, INVESTIGATE, or ESCALATE: how unusual the genome looks compared with everything we already know about. It is a cautious, transparent rule set — not a model making predictions — and it only escalates on novelty signals (the genome sitting unusually far from its closest known relative or its assigned family group), and only when the underlying data is good enough to trust.
    • Confidence — High / Medium / Low / Insufficient evidence: how solid the underlying data is, driven mainly by how much of each genome was successfully sequenced (coverage). Low confidence never pushes the assessment toward ESCALATE — it simply means "don't over-read this."
    • Transmission risk — short labels summarizing what the transmission analysis found for each query genome (e.g. sustained human transmission).
    • Narrative source — whether the paragraph below was drafted by the local AI assistant (ollama) or assembled from a fixed template (template). See Troubleshooting if you expected AI text.

    Two separate axes

    Assessment answers "how worrying is this genome?"; Confidence answers "how good is the data behind that call?" A clean, ordinary genome on solid data reads MONITOR · High confidence; a possibly-novel genome on thin data reads INVESTIGATE · Medium confidence. The rules almost never reach ESCALATE from identification evidence alone — that is deliberate.

    Step 2 — Scan the situation strip¶

    Four headline numbers summarize what was submitted — in the example above: 9 query genomes, 2 countries (Uganda + an unknown location), collection dates Jan 30–31, 2026, median coverage 98.9%:

    • Query genomes — how many submitted samples were identified as this pathogen.
    • Countries — where those samples were collected; Unknown appears when a sample's metadata didn't carry a location.
    • Collection dates — earliest to latest sampling date.
    • Median coverage — how completely the genomes were sequenced, on average; the main input to the Confidence badge.

    Step 3 — Read the narrative¶

    The blue-bordered paragraph is the generated situation summary: a plain-language synthesis of what the analysis found across identification, genomics, transmission, and the published evidence. In the example it ties the nine H3N2 genomes to their closest known reference (A/Washington/106/2016), reports the estimated R at sampling, and flags that the geographic link between the Ugandan samples and the matched U.S. transmission cluster is unclear — exactly the kind of caveat a reader should carry forward.

    Where it comes from

    Written during the analysis run and stored in intelligence_brief.json. When the local AI assistant is available it writes the paragraph; otherwise a fixed template fills in the same facts. Either way, every number in it traces back to a result table — it never contains a figure you can't find elsewhere in the dashboard.

    Step 4 — Walk the four evidence cards¶

    Below the narrative, four clickable cards summarize each layer of evidence — clicking one jumps straight to the full page for that question.

    What is it — identification. The closest known reference genome and how similar the query is to it, plus the closest matching past outbreak — in the example, H3N2 identified with 97.6% identity to A/Washington/106/2016 and a small genetic distance, i.e. a close match to a known, previously circulating strain. Click → Biological Threat.

    What's different about it — genomics. Whether the query carries more mutations than its background relatives (with a statistical significance check), which mutations are linked to known traits, and which proteins most distinguish it — plus two mini-plots:

    • Divergence boxplot — how far each query sits from the root of the family tree (red dots) compared with the background distribution. A query far above the box is unusually divergent.
    • Top proteins — the proteins whose mutations most separate the query from background genomes.

    Click → Pathogen Genomics.

    How it's spreading — transmission evidence. Whether the queries belong to known genetic clusters, where the lineage most likely originated, how many separate introductions the data suggests, and the estimated R at sampling — the average number of people each case infected at the time the samples were taken — plus a mini-map of where the query's relatives were collected.

    In the example below: 1 genetic cluster, likely origin USA, China; Kenya, R at sampling 3.48, and the map's dashed line connecting a U.S.-sampled relative to the Ugandan query — a visual hint of possible importation that the narrative also flags as uncertain.

    What we know & what we can do — evidence & countermeasures. How much published research yielded usable findings, how many evidence areas are covered, and the leading countermeasure product — plus an AI/auto highlights summary and two small charts.

    Here the card is deliberately honest about having nothing: 0/11 domains with evidence, no papers with claims, and a note that the evidence-summary step didn't run. Empty does not mean reassuring — it means the literature-evidence layer hasn't been computed for this species yet.

    Click → Evidence & Knowledge Gaps.

    Step 5 — Check the caveats¶

    The amber Caveats strip (visible at the bottom of the screenshot) lists the analysis' own warnings — here three important ones: 1 of 9 genomes failed identification QC (so the species call is provisional), no phenotype-linked mutation evidence, and no literature-evidence counts for this species. Read it before quoting any number further — it is the dashboard telling you exactly how far to trust what you just saw.

    Step 6 — The objectives strip¶

    Beneath the cards, a muted row of tiles mirrors the sidebar's objective list — a reminder of what else is available without competing with the assessment above.

    Public-health relevance¶

    This page is built for the 60-second read: a duty officer should be able to say "the analysis found H3N2, confidence is high, transmission evidence is moderate, here is the summary" — and know exactly which card to click for the supporting evidence. Everything shown is observed or estimated from data; the verdict is a transparent rule set, not a black box.

    Limitations

    • The assessment currently uses identification signals only; it is first-pass triage, not a full risk assessment.
    • "Insufficient evidence" means missing data, not low threat.
    • AI-drafted and template narratives differ in phrasing, not in facts.
